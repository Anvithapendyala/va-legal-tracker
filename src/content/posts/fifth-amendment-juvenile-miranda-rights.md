## Status & Jurisdiction

_J.D.B. v. North Carolina_, 564 U.S. 261 (2011), is a binding U.S. Supreme Court decision. Its Miranda rule applies nationwide, including Virginia. Virginia's juvenile-custody procedures may provide additional protections; the applicable rules depend on the young person's age, the setting, and the alleged offense.

## The Legal & Financial Rule

A 13-year-old student was questioned by a uniformed police officer and school administrators at school without being told he could leave or first receiving Miranda warnings. The Supreme Court held that a child's age must be considered when deciding whether a reasonable person in the child's position would have felt free to leave—if the age was known to the officer or would have been objectively apparent.

Miranda warnings are generally required before custodial interrogation. _J.D.B._ did not make every conversation with a student “custody,” nor does it mean a parent must always be present for questioning. Virginia law separately addresses taking a juvenile into custody and notification of a parent or guardian. [Virginia Code § 16.1-247](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title16.1/chapter11/section16.1-247/).

Source: [_J.D.B. v. North Carolina_](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/564/261/)

## Why It Matters to Your Rights & Wallet

Young people may experience questioning differently from adults, and that context can matter when a court evaluates custody and a statement's admissibility. If a school or police interview could involve alleged criminal conduct, families should ask whether the student is free to leave and contact a qualified Virginia juvenile-defense attorney before making decisions about a statement.
